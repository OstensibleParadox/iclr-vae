\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Gaussian noise is routinely used as a scalar control knob: choose a variance,
inject a perturbation, and tune its strength against predictive or generative
performance. This picture underlies feature-space regularization, stochastic
latent representations, neural operators, and diffusion models. It is reliable
at a fixed dimension, where every covariance matrix and Gaussian objective is
well defined. It becomes misleading when dimension represents resolution.
Refinement introduces additional modes, and a perturbation with unchanged
nominal strength can accumulate spectral mass in every newly resolved
direction. The same hyperparameter then ceases to describe the same geometric
intervention. A penalty intended to measure meaningful signal--noise geometry
can instead be dominated by the discretization cutoff, while its gradients
can transmit the same resolution dependence through the parameter-sensitivity
spectrum.

We make this failure precise through the relative covariance perturbation. Let
$C_N$ be a reference covariance at resolution $N$, let $C_N+\Delta C_N$ be its
perturbed counterpart, and define
\begin{equation}
  K_N = C_N^{-1/2}\Delta C_N C_N^{-1/2}.
  \label{eq:relative-covariance-perturbation}
\end{equation}
For $I+K_N\succ0$, the Gaussian covariance residue has the spectral form
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_N
  = \frac12\sum_{j\leq n_N}
    \left[\lambda_j(K_N)-\log\!\left(1+\lambda_j(K_N)\right)\right].
  \label{eq:gaussian-residue-intro}
\end{equation}
Because $\lambda-\log(1+\lambda)=\lambda^2/2+O(\lambda^3)$ near zero,
the unresolved tail is governed by squared spectral mass. This exposes the
finite-dimensional blind spot: every finite matrix belongs to every Schatten
class, so membership at one $N$ says nothing about refinement. The relevant
quantity is the dimension-uniform diagnostic
\begin{equation}
  \Ttwo(N) := \hsnorm{K_N}^{2},
  \qquad
  \sup_N \Ttwo(N) < \infty.
  \label{eq:t2-intro}
\end{equation}

This criterion leads directly to \emph{Schatten-filtered noise}. We apply a
spectral gate $K_N\mapsto K_N^{(\tau)}=\Phi_\tau(K_N)$ whose attenuation is
chosen to place the relative perturbation in a prescribed operator-ideal
regime uniformly over resolution. In the trace-class regime $\Schatten{1}$,
ordinary trace and log-determinant expressions remain available. In the larger
regime $\Schatten{2}\setminus\Schatten{1}$, those terms need not exist
separately, but their finite combination is captured by the
Carleman--Fredholm determinant,
\begin{equation}
  \mathcal L_N^{\mathrm{ren}}
  = -\frac12\log\det\nolimits_2(I+K_N).
  \label{eq:renormalized-objective-intro}
\end{equation}
Outside $\Schatten{2}$, the renormalized tail itself is not cutoff stable, so
filtering must alter the perturbation spectrum rather than merely rewrite the
objective. The method is therefore ideal-aware rather than simply
frequency-aware: it controls accumulated relative covariance geometry after
whitening, including anisotropy and spectral multiplicity.

Our main result identifies $\Schatten{2}$ as the sharp stability boundary.
Under dimension-independent positivity and spectral bounds, $\Ttwo(N)$ is
equivalent up to fixed constants to the Gaussian covariance residue, and it
exactly determines the variance of the centered sample log-density ratio.
Consequently, bounded $\Ttwo(N)$ yields an objective uniformly controlled
under refinement, whereas diverging $\Ttwo(N)$ forces unbounded cutoff growth.
For spectra
$s_j(K)\asymp j^{-\alpha}$, this gives a three-phase law: bounded behavior for
$\alpha>1/2$, logarithmic growth at $\alpha=1/2$, and polynomial growth for
$\alpha<1/2$.

Diffusion supplies the representation-level question. Latent diffusion should
not be viewed merely as computational compression: an encoder maps input
perturbations and defines the reference covariance relative to which downstream
Gaussian noise acts. Rather than presuming that compression supplies sufficient
regularity, we ask whether learned encoders control both relative covariance
families under refinement. Whitened Jacobian spectra, raw $\Schatten{2}$ mass,
and pixel-space, isometric, and learned-encoder controls make this hypothesis
falsifiable. Schatten filtering enforces the hidden requirement explicitly
instead of relying on representation compression to provide it implicitly.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{A sharp continuum-stability criterion.} We formulate Gaussian
  noise injection as a resolution-indexed relative covariance problem and
  identify Schatten-$2$ control as the cutoff-stability threshold, including
  the bounded, logarithmically critical, and polynomial power-law regimes.

  \item \textbf{An operator-ideal-aware noise mechanism.} We introduce
  Schatten-filtered noise together with the appropriate $\det_2$ objective in
  the Hilbert--Schmidt but non-trace-class regime. Its criterion targets
  cumulative covariance geometry rather than coordinate frequency alone.

  \item \textbf{Multi-scale tests connecting theory to learning.} Controlled
  spectra test the sharp boundary, operator-learning tasks test noise-scale
  transfer under grid refinement, and a Jacobian pushforward diagnostic asks
  whether latent encoders control tail $\Schatten{2}$ mass.
\end{itemize}
