\section{Gaussian Noise Under Refinement}
\label{sec:problem}

\subsection{Resolution-indexed perturbations}

Let $n_N$ denote the number of active coordinates or modes at resolution $N$,
with $n_N\to\infty$. We compare the centered Gaussian reference and its
noise-perturbed counterpart
\begin{equation}
  p_N = \mathcal N(0,C_N),
  \qquad
  q_N = \mathcal N(0,C_N+\Delta C_N).
\end{equation}
We assume $C_N\succ0$ and $C_N+\Delta C_N\succ0$ on the active subspace.
Whitening by $C_N$ gives the self-adjoint relative covariance perturbation
$K_N$ from \cref{eq:relative-covariance-perturbation}, so that $q_N$ has
relative covariance $I+K_N$. The eigenvalues of $K_N$ measure noise strength
relative to the reference geometry rather than in raw coordinates.

For finite $n_N$, the covariance contribution to the Gaussian KL divergence is
\begin{equation}
  \KL(q_N\Vert p_N)
  = \frac12\left(\operatorname{Tr}K_N-\log\det(I+K_N)\right)
  = \frac12\sum_{j\leq n_N}g(\lambda_{j,N}),
  \label{eq:finite-gaussian-kl}
\end{equation}
where $g(\lambda)=\lambda-\log(1+\lambda)$. Every term in
\cref{eq:finite-gaussian-kl} is finite at every fixed resolution. Stability is
therefore a property of the sequence $(K_N)_N$, not of any individual matrix.

Some implementations specify the same update through a relative precision
perturbation $H_N$,
\begin{equation}
  q_N=\mathcal N\!\left(
    0,C_N^{1/2}(I-H_N)^{-1}C_N^{1/2}
  \right),
  \qquad
  K_N=H_N(I-H_N)^{-1}.
  \label{eq:precision-covariance-map}
\end{equation}
Conversely, $H_N=K_N(I+K_N)^{-1}$. Under a dimension-independent positivity
gap and operator-norm bound, these spectral maps preserve uniform
$\Schatten{2}$ membership up to fixed constants. We state the theory in the
additive-covariance parameterization $K_N$, while using
\cref{eq:precision-covariance-map} to evaluate precision-parameterized noise
layers.

\subsection{How nominally small noise becomes large}

A scalar covariance rescaling already exhibits the issue. If
$C_N+\Delta C_N=aC_N$ for a fixed $a\neq1$, then
\begin{equation}
  K_N=(a-1)I_{n_N},
  \qquad
  \Ttwo(N)=(a-1)^2n_N.
  \label{eq:identity-direction}
\end{equation}
The perturbation is harmless at each cutoff but grows in every newly resolved
direction. In the infinite-rank limit, the identity-direction update is not
Hilbert--Schmidt; this is the operator-theoretic reason that scalar covariance
changes can leave a Gaussian equivalence class~\citep{bogachev1998gaussian}.
For learning, the immediate consequence is simpler: a nominal noise scale can
become a resolution-dependent intervention.

The same accumulation appears for decaying spectra. If
$s_j(K)\asymp j^{-\alpha}$, then the pointwise perturbation vanishes at high
frequency for every $\alpha>0$, but its cumulative squared mass may still
diverge. Thus ``low amplitude in every tail mode'' and ``stable under
refinement'' are different statements.

\subsection{The correct stability object}

We call a family of Gaussian perturbations \emph{uniformly refinement stable}
when its renormalized covariance objective and the $L^2$ norm of its centered
sample log-density ratio remain bounded uniformly over $N$. This notion is
uniform control, not by itself convergence of an arbitrary sequence of
cutoffs. The diagnostic is
\begin{equation}
  \Ttwo(N)=\operatorname{Tr}(K_N^\ast K_N)
  =\sum_{j\leq n_N}s_j(K_N)^2.
  \label{eq:t2-definition}
\end{equation}
This definition does not ask whether a finite matrix is Schatten: it asks
whether the Schatten mass stays bounded along the entire refinement path.

When $K_N\in\Schatten{1}$ uniformly, the trace and log determinant in
\cref{eq:finite-gaussian-kl} are separately uniformly controlled. For
compatible Galerkin cutoffs of a fixed trace-class operator, they also converge
separately. The larger
$\Schatten{2}$ regime requires their combined finite part,
\begin{equation}
  -\log\det\nolimits_2(I+K_N)
  = \operatorname{Tr}K_N-\log\det(I+K_N),
  \label{eq:det2-finite-cutoff}
\end{equation}
which is an exact identity at every finite cutoff. The distinction becomes
visible only as $N$ grows: $\Schatten{1}$ controls the two ordinary components,
whereas $\Schatten{2}$ controls their renormalized combination. In infinite
dimensions, $\det_2(I+K)$ is defined directly for $K\in\Schatten{2}$ with
$I+K\succ0$; \cref{eq:det2-finite-cutoff} must not be read as subtracting two
separately infinite quantities.
